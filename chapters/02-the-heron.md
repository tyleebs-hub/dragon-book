# Chapter 2: The Heron

*Hob*

Kell's Ford had two days to get ready and spent them the way it spent everything, badly and all at once.

The reeve, Hallam, who had been reeve for eleven years on the strength of being able to read, had Marran's two upstairs rooms stripped and the ticks beaten and the floors scrubbed with sand, and then had them done again because he had not been there to see it the first time. He had the street raked. Nobody could remember the street ever having been raked. He went down to the ferry house and told Hob's father to have the landing stage looked at, and Hob's father told him the landing stage had held a dozen bullocks on Tuesday and would hold a wizard, and Hallam went away unhappy. Somebody whitewashed the front of the church. Half of it, as it turned out, because the lime ran out and Jorrey the smith would not open a fresh barrel on the reeve's word alone.

Maddoc drilled them harder. He said nothing about why and nothing about the wizard and nothing at all about the staff, and the boys took their cue from him and said nothing either, within his hearing. Out of it they said a great deal.

"He'll want to see us fight," Dace said. "That's what it is. He'll sit there like a lord and have us go at each other and pick the one he likes."

"He'll want the spark," Ansel said. "That's what the school is. It's not for soldiers. My uncle said. They don't want boys who can fight, they want boys who can do the other."

"Then why's he going round the drill grounds?"

Ansel had no answer for that, and neither did anybody. Hob kept his mouth shut. He had been keeping it shut for two days and it was getting easier, which worried him.

He had told nobody what the rider said. Not his mother, not Jory, not the boys. He had told himself that it was because there was nothing to tell, that a man had asked a question and he had answered it, and that there was no reason the whole of Kell's Ford needed to know that Hob the ferryman's second son had said *yes sir, I am* to a stranger in the dusk and been told *maybe*. But he knew that was not why. He knew because of the other half of it, the half where he had not said Bram's name, and every time he thought about that half he felt the cold go through him again, and then something after the cold that was worse, which was that he would do it the same way if he had it over.

He did not look at Bram during the drills. He found he could feel where Bram was without looking, the way you could feel the fire in a room with your eyes shut.

---

On the second day his mother sent him down to the tannery with bread.

She did it the way she always did it, a loaf wrapped in a cloth put into his hands without a word while he was on his way out the door, and he took it the way he always took it, and went. Down the street, past Marran's and the half-white church, past the smithy, to where the houses stopped and the path went on alone to the pits.

The smell met him first. It always did. Lime and rot and something under both of them that was sweeter and worse, and the ground near the pits was grey and nothing grew in it, which should have made him think of the drill ground and did not, because the drill ground was clean. The tannery was a long low shed and a yard of pits dug into the clay, some covered, some not, and racks where the hides hung like the skins of men, and a house beyond it that was smaller than the shed.

He usually left the bread on the step. He had done it a hundred times. He stood on the step with the loaf in his hands and looked at the door, and then he went round the side of the house to the yard, because there were two days left and he wanted to see Bram's face when he said what he was going to say, and he did not know yet what he was going to say.

Bram was in the pits. He was up to his thighs in one of the uncovered ones, stripped to the waist, working a hide against the side of the pit with a long wooden paddle, and the liquor in the pit was the colour of weak tea and stank like the end of the world. His shoulders worked. There was a steadiness to it that was like the forms, the same movement over and over, the whole body behind each one. He did not look up. Tobin was nowhere. There was a sound of something being scraped from inside the shed.

"Brought bread," Hob said.

Bram looked up. He wiped his forearm across his face, which did nothing, since the forearm was wet to the elbow. "Step," he said.

"I know. I'll put it there." Hob did not move. "He's coming tomorrow. The wizard. Hallam says tomorrow, if the roads are good."

Bram went back to the paddle.

"They say he's looking for boys. For the school at Dunmere." Hob heard himself and could not stop. "Dace reckons he'll want to see us fight. Ansel's uncle says it isn't fighting he wants at all. Nobody knows. Maddoc won't say."

"Maddoc doesn't know."

"How do you know what Maddoc knows?"

Bram did not answer that. He worked the hide up the side of the pit, turned it, let it slide back. The liquor slopped. Hob stood on the grey ground with the bread going warm in his hands and the smell in the back of his throat and watched the biggest boy in Kell's Ford do the lowest work in it and felt, for a moment, something that was not jealousy and was not pity and was not anything he had a name for.

"You'll go," he said. "If he picks you. You'd have to."

"I'm not going anywhere."

"You don't even know what he wants."

Bram stopped. He stood in the pit with the paddle in both hands and the stuff running off him and looked at Hob, properly, for the first time since Hob had come round the house. His eyes did the thing they did. They went to Hob and stayed there, measuring.

"I know what he wants," he said.

Then he looked past Hob, at the shed, where the scraping had stopped, and Hob turned and saw Tobin in the doorway, a grey man in a leather apron with a curved knife in one hand and a face like something that had been left out in the weather, and Tobin looked at Hob and at the bread and at his son, and said nothing, and went back inside.

"Step," Bram said again, and bent to the hide.

Hob put the bread on the step. He walked back up the path with the smell on his clothes and the words going round in him. *I know what he wants.* He turned them over all the way home and by the time he got there he had decided what they meant, which was that Bram knew exactly how good he was and had always known, and had stood on the drill ground for four years letting the rest of them think they were in the same fight as him. That there had never been a bout Bram had not already won before it started. That he was not going anywhere because he did not need to go anywhere, because the thing the wizard was coming nine days up the Ferrin road to look for was a thing Bram already had and did not want, the way a rich man does not want the coin somebody drops in the street.

Hob was wrong about this too. But it did him a kindness, in its way. It made him angry, and the anger carried him through the night.

---

The carriage came the next day in the middle of the afternoon, and the whole village was in the street to see it, and it was worth seeing.

It was black, with the heron on the door in white, and it had four horses, which nobody in Kell's Ford had ever seen on one vehicle, big matched bays with their manes braided. The rider from the ferry was ahead of it on his lean horse. Two men rode behind, and these were soldiers, properly, in grey with the heron on their breasts and swords on their hips, and one of them was old enough to have grey in his beard and one of them was not much older than Jory and kept touching his sword-hilt as if to make sure it was there. The old one saw the village looking and looked back at it as if it were a field he had been told to hold. The young one grinned at a girl.

The carriage stopped outside Marran's. The driver got down and let the step down and opened the door. And Master Orvane of the king's school at Dunmere got out of it and stood in the raked street of Kell's Ford and looked around him with an expression of great and gentle interest, as a man might look at a litter of puppies.

Hob did not know what he had expected. Something tall. Something with a staff, or a beard to the waist, or robes. Orvane was none of these. He was a man of middling height and fifty years or so, and he was soft, not fat but soft, well-fed, well-kept, a man who had not carried anything heavier than a book in a long while. He wore black, good black, a long coat with a great deal of fine stitching at the collar, and there were rings on his hands. His beard was grey and trimmed close to the jaw. His hair was grey and there was not much of it. He had a round, pleasant, clever face, and he was smiling.

That was what Hob remembered afterwards. The smile. It was a warm smile. It was the smile of a man who was pleased to be here and pleased to see you and had already, in the moment of seeing you, worked out what you were worth, and had been right, and was pleased about that too. Hob had never wanted to hit a man in the face so badly in his life, and he could not for the life of him have said why, and he wanted, at the same time and just as badly, for the man to turn that smile on him and nod.

"Kell's Ford," Orvane said. His voice carried without being raised. "Reeve Hallam, I think? Fenn tells me you have been very kind. Thank you. No, please. No." Hallam had begun to bow and been stopped by two fingers. "We'll have none of that. I am a schoolmaster, not a duke. Mistress Marran. The rooms will be perfect, I am sure, and the ale better. Who is the sergeant?"

Maddoc was at the edge of the crowd, where he always was. He had not put on anything special. He stood with his bad leg planted and said, "Here."

Orvane looked at him. The smile did not go away but something under it went still, the way the river went still over a deep place.

"Sergeant," he said. "Of what?"

"Third foot."

"Third foot. Under Harrow, that would have been. At the Narrows."

"Yes."

"I was there," Orvane said. "After. I saw what was left." He let that sit for a moment, and the village did not know what it meant, and Hob did not know what it meant, but Maddoc knew, and his face did not move at all. "I would like very much to see your boys, Sergeant. Tomorrow, I think. I have been in that box since before dawn and I am too old to judge anything from inside it. Tonight, I would like to sit by a fire and drink Mistress Marran's ale and tell you all why I am here. Would that suit?"

It would suit. Kell's Ford would have agreed to anything he said, in that voice, with that smile. Hob watched him go in through Marran's door with Hallam fluttering behind him, and watched the soldiers see to the horses, and watched the rider, Fenn, swing down and stretch and look around and find Hob in the crowd and give him a small, dry nod.

Hob looked for Bram. Bram was not there. Of course he was not there. The whole village had come to see the heron on the door and Bram was in a pit up to his thighs in filth, and he did not care, and that was the worst of it.

---

Marran's was full that night in a way it had never been full. Every man in the village was in it and most of the women, standing when there was nowhere to sit, and children on shoulders and in the doorway and under the tables, and the fire built up high though it was not cold, and the smell of too many people and too much ale and the heat. Hob was wedged into the corner by the barrel with Dace on one side and the wall on the other, and he could see the fire and the chair by it, and the man in the chair.

Orvane did not stand to speak. He sat with a cup in his hand and his legs crossed at the ankle and spoke as if he were talking to three friends, and the room leaned in to hear him and went quiet enough to hear the fire.

"You will have heard," he said, "that the king is afraid of dragons."

Somebody laughed. Orvane smiled at the laugh as if he had been hoping for it.

"Yes. Everyone laughs. I laughed myself, once, when I was young and knew things. Let me ask you this. Who here has seen a dragon?"

Nobody had. A few hands went half up and came down again. Old Garan at the back said his father had, once, in the hills past Ferrin, a long way off, asleep on a rock in the sun like a cat.

"Like a cat," Orvane said. "Yes. That is exactly what they are like, nearly all of them, nearly all of the time. They find a hole in a hill and a few things that shine and they lie down on them, and they lie there for two hundred years, and if you leave them alone they will leave you alone, and the only harm they ever do is to the sheep. I have met four. Three of them I could have walked up to and slapped. That is what a dragon is, to a man like your father, Garan, and to a king, and to most of the wizards I have had the misfortune to sit in council with." He drank. "And that is the whole of the trouble."

He told them, then. He told it well. He told it the way Maddoc tapped an elbow, without any waste, and Hob, who had spent his life being told things by people who did not know how, felt it go into him like water into dry ground.

A dragon was born small and stupid and stayed that way for a long time. Then it was not small. Then there was a season, a few years in a life of centuries, when it was neither young nor old, and in that season it was something else entirely. It wanted. It wanted the next hill and the hill past that. It wanted what the other dragons had and it took it. It burned what was in the way. Men who had lived in a valley under a dragon's hill for six generations and never lost more than a ewe woke one night and the valley was gone. This had happened. There were records. It had happened in Caldreth within the memory of men still living, twice, and it had happened in other kingdoms more than that, and every time it happened the men who survived it said the same thing, which was that nobody had told them.

"Someone had told them," Orvane said. "Someone always has. They did not listen, because the dragon on the hill had been asleep for two hundred years and looked like a cat."

He paused. He looked around the room, and Hob had the strange feeling that he was counting it.

"Now. Suppose I told you that there are a great many dragons on a great many hills, and that a great many of them hatched within a few years of one another, a long time ago, for reasons that are a matter of some dispute among people who dispute for a living. And suppose I told you that the season I have described does not come at a fixed age, like a boy's beard, but near enough to one that a man who could count might make a guess. And suppose I told you what that man's guess was."

He did not tell them. He let it sit.

"The king believes it," he said. "His father did not. His father's wizards did not, and there was a time when I did not, and I have spent twenty years and a great deal of the king's money being wrong about that in the other direction, and I will go on doing it. Because if I am wrong, what have we lost? A school. Some boys taught to do something strange with their hands who might otherwise have been farmers. And if I am right." He smiled. "Well. If I am right, you will want to have been nice to me."

The room laughed. It was a relief to laugh. Orvane let them, and drank, and when the laugh had gone by he said, in the same voice, "Mistress Marran, might I trouble you to put out the candles?"

Marran did not understand him. He said it again, patiently, and she went round with her fingers and did it, and the room grew dimmer by stages, and people shifted, and then there was only the fire.

"Thank you. Now." He set his cup down. He held up his right hand, and did nothing with it, and the fire went out.

It did not go down. It went out, between one moment and the next, the way a candle goes when you pinch it, and the room was black, and in the black there was a noise from forty people that was not a word, and a child began to cry. Hob could not see his own hand. He could feel Dace's arm against his, rigid. He could hear the wizard breathing, slow and even, in the dark.

"This is what a dragon is," Orvane said, quietly, from somewhere. "Not fire. The other thing. The moment after. You have had your fire for two hundred years and you have forgotten it could be taken."

Nothing happened for a long time. Perhaps it was not a long time.

Then there was a small sound, a snap, and a flame stood on the end of Orvane's finger, one small yellow flame like a candle's, and his face above it was lit from below and smiling, and he leaned down and touched it to the wood in the hearth, and the fire caught, and came up, and the room came back with it, and people breathed.

Hob was watching the hand. He did not know why. Everyone else was watching the fire and he was watching the hand that had lit it, and he saw it shake. It was a small shake, a tremor, and Orvane put the hand down on his knee at once and covered it with the other one, and picked up his cup with his left, and smiled, and nobody saw but Hob.

"Which brings me," Orvane said, "to why I am here."

The spark. He called it that, the way Ansel's uncle had. He said it was in perhaps one child in a thousand, and in most of those it was so faint it did nothing but make them clumsy, and in a very few it was enough to be trained, and that the king's school at Dunmere existed to find those few and train them before the season he had spoken of came round. He said a child with the spark did not know he had it, as a rule. He said other people knew, if they knew what to look for.

"Things break," he said. "That shouldn't. A good tool, a sound piece of wood, a pot that has been on the fire a hundred times. Near him, when his blood is up. Animals don't like him. Dogs, especially. Milk turns. And there is a feeling, if you are close. You will know it if you have had it, though you will have called it something else." He touched his own jaw. "Your teeth ache. There is a taste."

Hob stopped breathing. It was not a decision. His body did it.

"So I ask," Orvane said, "in every village I come to, and I ask it here. Has anything like that happened in Kell's Ford? To anyone's knowledge? Anything odd. Anything you told yourselves was nothing."

The room was quiet. Hob could hear his own heart. He could feel the eyes of the village moving, not toward him, not toward anyone, moving the way eyes did when nobody wanted to be first. He kept his own on the fire. He had done this once. He could do it again. It was easier the second time. It was so much easier the second time that he was frightened by it, and his mouth stayed shut, and the moment went on, and he thought: it is going to pass. He is going to say *well then* and the moment is going to pass.

"Bram's staff," Dace said.

He said it the way Dace said everything, without having decided to. He had had two cups of Marran's ale and he was fifteen and the dark had frightened him and he wanted to say something true in a room full of grown men, and he said it, and then he heard it, and his face went slack.

Orvane turned his head. "Whose?"

"Bram's. He's. He's one of us. Sergeant's. His staff broke. Three days back. Right down the middle." Dace swallowed. "Ash. It wasn't even a hard hit."

"Who was he fighting?"

And the eyes went to Hob. All of them. Dace's, and Pim's across the room, and Wat's, and Maddoc's from the door, and his father's, and his mother's, and the wizard's, which were pale and interested and had already, Hob could see, worked out the answer before anybody opened their mouth.

"Me, sir," Hob said.

"Ah." Orvane looked at him. The smile did not change. "The ferry boy. Fenn mentioned you. You told him you were good."

"Yes, sir."

"And are you?"

The room waited. Hob's face burned. He thought of a dozen things to say and said, "I was standing up when it broke, sir."

Orvane laughed. It was a real laugh, short and surprised, and the room laughed with it because the wizard had, and the heat went out of Hob's face and left something colder behind.

"So you were," Orvane said. "Good. That's good. And this Bram. Where is he? I don't see him. I think I would see him."

"Working," said Maddoc, from the door.

"At this hour?"

"Tanner's boy."

"Ah," said Orvane again, differently. He looked at Maddoc for a moment and Maddoc looked back, and whatever passed between them passed, and Orvane turned to the room with the smile back in place. "Then I'll see him tomorrow. I'll see all of you tomorrow, all of the sergeant's boys, on the sergeant's ground. And Sergeant." He raised his cup a little. "I'd like to see them fight."

---

Hob did not sleep. He lay on the floor and did not sleep and heard the house breathe around him and the river go by, and at some point past the middle of the night he got up and went out, quietly, past his grandmother who was not asleep either and said nothing, and stood in the street in his shirt.

Marran's was dark. The carriage stood in the yard beside it with the heron grey in the starlight. There was a light in one upstairs window, low, a candle, and as he watched it a shadow crossed it, a man's, and stopped, and he thought absurdly that the wizard was looking down at him, and knew he could not be, and stood there anyway.

Then there was another shadow in the street and it was Maddoc.

He came down from the top of the street with his stiff leg, slowly, the way he walked when there was nobody to see, and he did not see Hob in the dark by the wall until he was nearly on him, and then he stopped.

"Go home," he said.

"Can't sleep."

"Then lie there and don't sleep. Go home."

Hob did not go. Maddoc looked at him, and then up at the lit window, and then back at him, and something in the old man's face gave a little, the way the river bank did in spring, a small slip, not the whole thing.

"You want him to pick you," Maddoc said.

"Yes, Sergeant."

"He won't."

Hob had known that. He had known it since the fire went out. Hearing it was different. He stood with his back to the wall and his arms folded against the cold and said, "He might. He hasn't seen me."

"He's seen you. He saw you tonight. He saw all of us." Maddoc coughed and turned his head and spat. "He's not here for boys who can fight, lad. He's here for the other thing. You know who's got it. Everybody in that room knows who's got it, and I've known for three years, and I've kept my mouth shut for three years, and tonight Dace Miller opened his for me." He said this without heat. "So that's done."

"You've known."

"Since the pot."

Hob did not know about any pot. He wanted to ask and did not. He said instead, "You told him never the head."

Maddoc looked at him. "Pim," he said.

"Why?"

The old man was quiet a long time. The candle in the window went out. The street was very dark.

"I've seen it before," Maddoc said at last. "What that does, with nobody to teach it. Once. That's enough." He shifted his weight off the bad leg. "He'll go with the wizard. His father'll let him go because his father's not a fool, whatever else he is, and the village'll be glad, and they'll be right to be. And you'll stand on my ground tomorrow and do your forms and go home and in a year or two the levy will come up the road and you'll go with it, and you'll come back, because I've seen to that. That's what I can do for you. It's not nothing."

"No, Sergeant."

"Go home, Hob."

Hob went home. He lay down on the floor with his brother's foot in his back and did not sleep, and said the words, and they meant what they always meant, and he held on to them.

---

In the morning the whole village stood at the fence of the drill ground, and the wizard sat in Marran's best chair at the top of it with Fenn standing behind him and the two soldiers at the gate, and the eight boys of Kell's Ford went through their forms on the dead ground in a line, high guard, low guard, the step and strike, while the sun came up white behind the willows.

Hob did the best forms of his life. He knew it while he was doing it. His staff was where it should be a hair before it should be there, and his feet were right, and his breath was right, and he could feel the line of his own body from heel to hand like a drawn rope. Maddoc walked the line and did not tap him once.

Orvane was watching Bram.

He had been watching Bram from the moment Bram came up the path, last, from the downwind end, with the smell on him and the new staff in his hand. He had not stopped. He sat in the chair with his soft hands folded on his knee and his pleasant face very still and watched the biggest boy in Kell's Ford go through the fifth form, and the sixth, and his eyes did not go anywhere else, and Hob, who was doing the best forms of his life six feet away, might as well have been a post.

Bram did his forms the way he did everything. He looked at the ground a yard in front of his feet.

When the forms were done Maddoc brought them to a halt and stood them in their line and turned to the chair, and said nothing, and waited.

"Thank you, Sergeant," Orvane said. "That was very correct." He said *correct* the way a man says *small*. He stood up, and stretched, and looked along the line, and when his eyes came to Hob they stopped for a moment, and the smile warmed, and Hob felt his heart go up into his throat.

"The ferry boy," Orvane said. "Fenn's friend. You are good. You will make a sergeant, if the levy doesn't kill you first, and I don't think it will. Well done." He had already moved on. "Now. I would like to see the big one."

The village made a sound. Hob did not hear it. He was hearing *you will make a sergeant*, over and over, in that warm voice, and it was not the words, it was the kindness. The man had meant it. He had meant it as a gift.

"Pairs, Sergeant," Orvane said. "I don't care who else. I want to see him fight. Who fights him?"

Maddoc did not answer at once. He looked at the ground. He looked at Bram, who had not moved and whose eyes were on that spot of earth a yard in front of his boots. He looked at the wizard. He had a face, Hob thought, like a man being asked to hand over something he had carried a long way.

"Who fights him best," Orvane said, gently. "Sergeant."

Maddoc looked at Hob.

He did not want to. Hob saw that. He saw the old man not want to, and look anyway, because there was no one else to look at and because it was the truth, and the truth was the only thing Maddoc had ever given any of them. Hob had been on the ground twice three days ago and got up twice and been standing when the staff broke, and in four years nobody else in Kell's Ford had ever been standing at the end.

"Hob," Maddoc said.

Hob stepped out of the line.

He walked to the middle of the dead ground and turned to face the downwind end, and Bram came out to meet him, and they stood a staff's length apart the way they had stood thirty times before. The village was at the fence. The wizard was in his chair. The sky was white. Hob could smell the tannery on the boy in front of him and he could hear the river through the willows, the sound you stopped hearing after a week and never heard again unless you went away.

Bram looked at the ground a yard in front of his feet.

Hob thought: he has already decided. He has already worked out how this goes. He thought: let him. Let him have worked it out. He can take my wind and my footing and my staff and he can put me on this ground as many times as he likes in front of the king's own wizard, and there is one thing he cannot take, and I am going to show them what it is.

Never stay down.

"Go," said Maddoc.
