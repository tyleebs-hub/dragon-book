# Chapter 3: Never Stay Down

*Hob*

He went in careful.

He had learned that much, three days ago, and he held on to it the way he held on to the phrase: go in careful, make him work, make it a bout and not a lesson. He came in with his staff low and his weight back and he feinted at the knee and came up at the ribs and Bram's staff was there, the way it was always there, with that patient knocking sound, and Hob let it be there and stepped off and came again from the other side.

They traded. It was good. He knew it was good because he could hear the fence: the small sound a crowd makes when it is watching something it did not expect. Dace's voice in it. Pim's. He did not look. He kept his eyes on Bram's chest, where the movement started, because the eyes told you nothing and the feet told you nothing and if there was a tell anywhere on that big flat body it was in the chest, a breath taken a hair early, and he had never found it in four years and he was looking for it now.

Bram looked at the ground a yard in front of his own feet, and let Hob come, and took each strike on the ash, and gave nothing.

Then he gave something and Hob was down.

A sweep. He thought it was a sweep. His feet were out from under him and his shoulder hit the dead ground and his staff went skittering and the sky swung over him white and enormous and then stopped. His hip again. The same hip. He lay for the space of one breath, which was all he allowed himself, and listened to the fence let its breath out, and heard, from the chair at the top of the ground, nothing at all.

He got up.

Never stay down. He found his staff. He set his feet. Bram had not moved from where he stood; he never did, he never pressed, he never came to finish you, he waited for you to come back and you came. Hob came back.

"Again," said Maddoc, as if it were any morning.

---

The second time was harder.

He went in careful again and Bram did not let him be careful. Bram came forward. That was new, and Hob did not know what to do with it, and while he was not knowing Bram's staff hit his left forearm with a crack that went up into his shoulder and his own staff dropped out of that hand and hung from the other, and Bram's staff came round and took him in the ribs on the right side, not hard, Hob would have said not hard, and the air went out of him like water out of a kicked bucket and he was on his knees and then on his face.

Dirt. The taste of the dead ground, which tasted of nothing, which was the taste of a thousand mornings. He could not breathe. He tried and his body would not do it and for a long moment there was only the trying, and then the breath came back in a thin whistling string and he lay on his face and pulled it in and heard the fence, which had gone quiet, and heard his mother's voice say his name, once, not loud.

He got up.

It took longer. His left arm would not close properly on the staff and he closed it anyway. His right side was a single hot plank from armpit to hip. He stood and swayed and set his feet and looked at Bram, and Bram was not looking at the ground.

Bram was looking at the chair.

It was quick. It was a flick of the eyes, up to the top of the ground where the wizard sat with his soft hands folded, and back. But Hob saw it, and something in him went cold and bright at once, because he understood it. Bram was being watched and Bram knew it. Bram, who had never in four years given anything to anybody on this ground, was giving the wizard a show. He had come forward because the wizard wanted to see him come forward. He had hit Hob's arm, which he did not need to hit, because the wizard wanted to see what it looked like when he hit something.

So that was how it was. Hob thought: all right. You want to show him. I want to show him too. We will both show him, then, and we will see who he looks at.

"That's enough," Maddoc said.

He said it from the side of the ground, where he stood with his bad leg planted. He said it the way he said *form up* and *fetch the water*, flat, and for a moment nothing happened, because nobody on that ground had ever heard him say it before and did not know what it meant.

"No," said Orvane. "Let them go on."

He did not raise his voice. He did not stand. He said it pleasantly from Marran's best chair with his legs crossed at the ankle and his eyes on Bram, and the two soldiers at the gate shifted their feet, and Fenn behind the chair looked at the back of the wizard's head and then at the ground.

"Master Orvane."

"I want to see, Sergeant. That is what I came for. The boy is standing. Aren't you, boy?"

"Yes, sir," said Hob.

"There. Go on."

Maddoc looked at Hob. Hob did not look back. He was looking at Bram, and he was thinking: he wants to see. The king's own wizard, nine days up the Ferrin road, wants to see, and I am standing, and he asked me and not you.

"Go on, then," Maddoc said, and his voice had something in it that was not flat, and Hob did not hear it.

---

He changed his guard.

He had thought of it at some point during the night, lying on the floor with his brother's foot in his back and the candle going out in Marran's window, and he had put it away as a thing you did not do, and he took it out now. He dropped his staff to a low guard, lower than low, the end of it nearly on the ground, so that everything above his belt was open, his chest, his throat, his face, and he came in that way.

It was madness against anyone else. Against anyone else it was asking to have your skull opened. But Bram did not hit the head. Bram had not hit a head in three years, since Maddoc took him aside and said whatever he had said, and if the only thing Bram would not do was the thing Hob left open, then Hob had taken away half of him, and the other half was ribs and legs and Hob had already been hit in the ribs and legs and was standing.

He saw Bram see it. The eyes came up off the ground and went to Hob's face, bare and offered, and stayed there a moment, and Hob saw something go across the flat features that he had never seen there before and did not have a name for, and then Bram's staff came in low, at the thigh, where it had to, and Hob took it and stayed up and hit him.

He hit him. Ash on ribs, a solid thudding sound, the first clean blow Hob had landed on Bram in four years, and the fence made a noise like a wave breaking, and Hob felt it go up his arms into his heart.

Then Bram took his leg out from under him and he was down a third time, on his back, and the breath went again.

He got up. He was laughing, or something like it. He could not feel his left hand. He got up.

"Stay down," Bram said.

Hob stopped.

It was the first thing Bram had said since *go*. It was the first thing Bram had ever said in a bout, in any bout, to anyone. He said it low and flat, the way he said *step*, and he was looking at Hob's face, not the ground, and his chest was going up and down, and Hob thought, with a clarity that was almost pleasure: he's breathing hard. I have made him breathe hard.

"No," Hob said.

"Hob. Stay down."

"That's not the rule."

He went in with his face open.

---

He did not know, afterwards, in the time he had afterwards, how many more there were. Two. He thought two. They ran together. He knew that he hit Bram once more, on the arm, and that it was a good one. He knew that he went down once onto his bad hip and lay there for longer than a breath, long enough to hear the fence properly, and that it was not making the wave sound any more. It was making a sound like one voice, low, and the voice was saying *stop*, in several different ways. Dace's was in it. Pim's was in it. Hallam the reeve was saying *Sergeant* over and over as if the word were a rope he could pull. His mother was not saying anything. He could feel where she was at the fence the way he could feel where Bram was, like the fire in a room with your eyes shut, and she was not saying anything, and that was the worst sound of all.

"Master Orvane," Maddoc said again. He was closer. He had come onto the ground. "I am stopping this."

"You will stop it when I have seen what I need to see, Sergeant, and not before." The wizard's voice had not changed. It was warm and interested and reasonable, and it came from very far away. "I think we are close. Don't you? Look at him."

Hob did not know which of them the wizard meant. He got up.

He got up and it was like climbing out of the river in winter, the way the body did not want to and did it, and he stood and could not find the staff and then found it in his right hand where it had been all along, and he lifted it, and his left arm came up to join it and would not close and he let it hang. He set his feet. He looked at Bram.

Bram was not looking at him. Bram was looking at his own hand.

It was his right hand, the one at the top of the staff, and he was looking at it the way he had looked at it three days ago over the two halves of a broken ash stave, as if someone had put it there while he was not paying attention, and his face was wrong. Hob had never seen Bram's face wrong. It was grey under the tannery brown and the mouth was open a little and the eyes were not measuring anything. They were just open.

Hob tasted iron.

He tasted it before anything happened. He tasted it standing still, the smithy taste, the struck-bar taste, and his teeth began to ache as if he had bitten down on a spoon, and his ears filled with a high thin ringing like a struck bowl, and the hair stood up along his arms, and the whole dead ground seemed to lean toward the boy at the centre of it the way a field of wheat leans before the wind comes over the hill.

He thought: that's him. That's the thing. That is what the wizard came to see.

He thought: I am standing. Look at me.

He went in.

Bram's staff came up to meet him and it was not a hard blow. Hob would have sworn it before God and the king. It was not fast and it was not hard and it came in at his right side, where the ribs were, where everything already was, the way every blow of Bram's had come in for three years because Bram did not hit the head, and Hob saw it come and could have got his staff there and did not, because he had decided, he had finally decided something the way Bram decided things, and what he had decided was that he would take it and stand.

He took it.

The sound was the sound the staff had made. The long crack going on longer than it should, like river ice in spring. But it was not the staff. His own staff split in his right hand, he felt it go, felt the two halves of it turn to splinters and leave him, but that was not the sound. The sound was inside him. Something along his right side went the way the ash had gone, along its whole length, and the ringing in his ears became everything, and the taste of iron became everything, and the white sky came down and hit him in the back.

---

He was on the ground.

He knew that. He could feel the dead earth under him, flat and cool and tasting of nothing. He could see the sky. It was very white and very close now, close enough to touch, and there were the tops of the willows at the edge of it, moving.

Get up.

He told his legs. They did not do it. He told them again, the way you tell a dog, and nothing came back from them, not refusal, nothing. He told his right arm and it moved, a little, the hand opening and closing on splinters. His left arm he could not find at all.

Never stay down.

He thought it very clearly. The words were there, whole, the way they were every morning, and they meant what they had always meant, that there was one thing they could not take, and he reached for it and found that it was not where he had left it. It was not in the words. It had never been in the words. It had been in the legs, all along, in the dumb animal getting up of the body, and the body had been the thing he owned, the only thing, and it was not answering.

He breathed in and the breath did not go where breath went. It went somewhere else, somewhere on the right side, with a sound he could hear, a wet pulling sound, and when he breathed out there was blood in his mouth and it was not from his tongue. It was warm and it kept coming. He turned his head to let it out and it ran down into the dirt and the dirt took it the way it took everything, without any mark.

Faces.

Maddoc's first, over him, upside down, the old man on his bad knee in the dirt with his three-fingered hand flat on Hob's chest, not pressing, just there, the way you put a hand on a horse. His mouth was moving. Hob could not hear him. The ringing was going away now and under it was the other sound, the river, through the willows, the sound you stopped hearing after a week in the village and never heard again unless you went away. He could hear it. He could hear it very well.

Then his mother. He could not see her but he could feel her, the way he always could, and she had come onto the ground, and there was a sound coming from her that he had never heard a person make, and he wanted to tell her it was all right, he was standing, he had been standing, the wizard had seen him standing, and the blood came up instead and he let it.

Then the wizard. Standing. He had stood up out of the chair at last and come down the ground and he was looking down at Hob with his pleasant round face, and the face was interested. That was all. Not sorry. Not glad. Interested, the way it had been interested in the litter of puppies that was Kell's Ford, and then, while Hob watched, it arranged itself into something else, something grave and kind, like a man putting on a coat.

And then Bram.

He had come without Hob seeing him come. He was on his knees on the other side from Maddoc, and he was close, closer than he had ever been, close enough that Hob could smell the lime and the pits and under it just a boy's sweat, and his face was over Hob's face, and Hob looked at it.

He had looked at that face for four years. He had looked at it across a staff's length thirty times and across the drill ground a thousand mornings and across the river at the roots of the willow and across a stinking pit with a loaf of bread going warm in his hands, and every time he had seen the same thing, which was a boy who had already decided. Who had measured you and worked you out and was only waiting to carry it through. Hob had built his whole idea of the world on that face. He had been afraid of it and he had wanted to be it and he had kept his mouth shut about it twice to the king's own men, and he had come in with his own face open against it this morning because he had known, he had known for certain, what it would and would not do.

He looked at it now from a foot away with the blood going out of him and saw it for the first time.

It had not decided anything. It had never decided anything. It was a boy's face, sixteen, grey, with the mouth open and the eyes gone wide and wet and nothing behind them but a kind of enormous surprise, and it was looking at Hob the way it had looked at the broken staff and at its own hand, as if somebody had done this while it was not paying attention, and it did not know who.

He was not sure of anything, Hob thought. He never was. All this time.

He wanted to say it. He wanted to say *I was wrong about you*, which seemed suddenly like the most important thing he had ever had to say to anybody, more important than *I am* to a stranger in the dusk, and he got his mouth around the beginning of it and what came out was blood, and Bram flinched from it as if it had been a blow.

"Hob," Bram said. His voice was not flat. "Hob. Get up."

Hob would have laughed if there had been anything to laugh with. He thought: that's the rule. He thought: you had it the other way round. The whole time. I never knew.

His right hand found something and closed on it. It was Bram's wrist. He did not know whether he had meant it to be. He held it, and felt the pulse going in it, fast, a boy's pulse, and he thought that was all right. That was something to hold.

The sky was very white. The willows moved at the edge of it. The river went on through them, the sound he had not heard since he was a small boy, and he lay on the dead ground where nothing grew and listened to it, and it was louder now, and closer, and it was the only thing, and he was going away, he was going at last, and he was not afraid of it, and he did not stay.
