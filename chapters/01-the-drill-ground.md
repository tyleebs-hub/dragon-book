# Chapter 1: The Drill Ground

*Hob*

The ground behind Maddoc's house had been beaten so flat and for so long that nothing grew on it, not even in spring. Hob liked that about it. Everything else in Kell's Ford grew whether you wanted it to or not: the reeds, the nettles along the ferry path, his brothers. The drill ground stayed what it was.

He was first, as usual. He stood in the grey light with his staff across his shoulders and watched his breath go up and come apart. Beyond the willows the river made its low, constant noise, the sound you stopped hearing after a week in the village and never heard again unless you went away. Hob had never gone away. He had been to the market at Ferrin, nine miles down the road, three times.

A door scraped behind him and he straightened without meaning to.

"You're early," Maddoc said.

"Yes, Sergeant."

"Early is what you do when you've got nothing better." Maddoc coughed, worked something loose, and spat into the dead grass at the edge of the ground. "Fetch the water."

Hob fetched the water. Two buckets from the pump at the side of the house, carried out to the post at the far end where the boys would drink between bouts, and the old man watched him carry them with the look he gave everything, as if he were deciding whether it would hold weight. Maddoc had three fingers on his left hand and a right leg that did not bend the way it should. He had gone to the king's wars at eighteen and come back at thirty with a sergeant's pay and a bag of coin that had bought the house and the land behind it, and in the twenty years since, as far as Hob could tell, he had done nothing but train the boys of Kell's Ford to hit each other with sticks. He never spoke about the wars. The boys had theories. Dace said he had killed a hundred men and Wat said he had killed one and never got over it, and Hob thought both of them were wrong, because neither of those men would have come home and done this.

"Form up," Maddoc said, though there was nobody to form up but Hob. "Go on. Don't wait for the rest of them."

So Hob went through the forms alone while the light came. High guard, low guard, the step and strike, the step and strike, the turn. Fourth form, fifth. The staff was ash, as long as he was tall and a little more, worn smooth where his hands went. His father had cut it for him when he was twelve and he had grown into it. He could feel Maddoc not watching him, which was how Maddoc watched.

The others came in ones and twos as the sky went from grey to white. Dace, loud before he was through the gate. Wat and Pim, who were brothers and looked it and argued about everything, including which of them was older, which was not a thing a person could argue about. Ansel, small and quick, who would have been the best of them if he had weighed anything. Three more. Eight boys, counting Hob, which was every boy in Kell's Ford between fourteen and seventeen who could be spared from work, and two who could not and came anyway.

Bram came last. He always did.

He came up the path from the downwind end of the village where the tannery was, and Hob could smell him before he could see him properly, lime and old blood and the sweet rot of hides, the smell that had been on Bram since he was nine years old and had gone to work in the pits with his father. Nobody stood close to him. It was not unkindness, exactly. It was the smell, and it was the other thing, and the other thing was harder to say.

He was a hand taller than anyone else on the ground and he was wide through the shoulders the way a door is wide, flat and solid. He carried his staff in one hand like it weighed nothing. He had a plain face that did not move much. His eyes moved. That was what Hob had noticed, years ago, and never stopped noticing: Bram's eyes went to a thing and stayed on it and you could see him working, turning something over, measuring, and then he would do whatever he did. Hob had decided a long time ago that this was what it looked like when a person was deciding things. Everybody else in the village just did things. Bram decided them.

"Morning," Hob said, because somebody should.

Bram looked at him. Nodded. Took his place at the end of the line.

---

They went through the forms together and Maddoc walked the line and corrected with the end of his own staff, a tap on an elbow, a tap behind a knee. He did not talk much when he corrected. He talked when he was angry and he talked at the start and the end, and in between he tapped.

When the forms were done he stood in front of them with his bad leg planted and his good one behind, the way he always stood, and said what he always said.

"What do we do?"

"Never stay down," eight boys said.

"What do we do?"

"Never stay down."

"Why?"

That one had an answer too, and Hob said it with the rest, but he was not really saying it. He was thinking about it. He thought about it most mornings, standing on the dead ground with the words in his mouth, because it seemed to him the truest thing anyone had ever told him, and nobody had told it to him, exactly. Maddoc had said it to the line and Hob had been part of the line.

Everything could be taken from you in a fight. Your wind. Your footing. Your weapon. Your plan, if you had been fool enough to make one. The other man could be stronger and faster and meaner and there was nothing you could do about any of that, but there was one thing he could not take, which was whether you got up. The ground was where a fight ended only if you let it be. You could lose a fight and still never stay down, and if you never stayed down then in the way that mattered you had not lost. Hob believed this. He believed it the way he believed the river would be there in the morning. It was the only thing he owned that nobody could argue him out of.

"Because down is where they kill you," said eight boys, and Maddoc nodded as if this was news.

"Pairs," he said. "Dace, Ansel. Wat, Pim." That got a groan from both of them and a flat look from Maddoc that ended it. He went down the line. He got to Hob and his eyes went past him to the end, and Hob knew before he said it.

"Hob. Bram."

"Oh, lovely," Dace said, under his breath. "Somebody fetch the priest."

"Dace," Maddoc said.

"Sergeant."

"You'll go first. With me."

Dace's grin went away. Hob did not watch what happened to him next, though he heard it, the clack of ash on ash and then a sound like a sack of meal going down and Dace saying something that was not a word. He was looking at Bram, and Bram was looking at the ground about a yard in front of his own feet, which was where he always looked before a bout.

Hob had fought Bram perhaps thirty times in four years. He had never won. Nobody had ever won, as far as he knew, not even Maddoc, who had stopped going against Bram two summers ago and had not said why. The thing about Bram was not that he was fast, though he was faster than a man that size should be. It was that he did not seem to do anything you could see coming. He stood very still, and then he had hit you, and in between there was nothing, no shift of the feet, no tell in the shoulders, nothing you could learn. Hob had tried to learn it. He had lain awake trying to learn it. He had decided, finally, that there was nothing to learn because Bram was not reacting to anything. He had already worked out what he was going to do before the bout began. He had looked at you and measured you and made his decision and the fight was just him carrying it out.

That was the frightening part. Not the hitting. The deciding.

"Hob," Maddoc said. "Bram. Go."

Hob went.

He went in low, because going in high against Bram was asking to have your hands broken, and he feinted left and came right and got the end of his staff about a foot from Bram's ribs before it stopped, because Bram's staff was there. He had not seen it move. He never saw it move. He stepped back and reset and tried again, a proper exchange this time, three strikes, high, low, high, and Bram took each one on his own staff with a sound like someone knocking on a door, patient, and did nothing else.

Then he did something else and Hob was on his back.

He did not know how he had got there. His hip hurt and his ears were full of his own blood and the sky was very white and very far. He got up. His legs did it before he told them to. Never stay down. He got up and found his staff and brought it round and Bram was standing where he had been, waiting, looking at that spot on the ground.

"Again," said Maddoc, from somewhere.

Hob went again. He was angry now, which he knew was a mistake and could not help. He went in straight and fast and hit Bram's staff so hard his hands sang and did it again and did it again and for a moment he thought he had him, Bram was giving ground, a step, another step, and then something took Hob's leg out from under him and he went down on his face and bit his tongue.

Blood in his mouth. Dirt in his teeth. He got up.

"Never stay down," he said, to nobody, through the blood, and some of the boys laughed, and it was not a bad laugh. Pim said "Go on, Hob," and Hob went on.

The third time was different. He knew it was different while it was happening and could not have said how. He came in careful, the way he should have come in the first time, and Bram met him, and they traded, and it was almost like a real bout, like two people who were trying. Hob's arms burned. He could hear himself breathing. And then Bram's staff came down at him from high on the right, not fast, not even hard, Hob would have sworn to that for the rest of his life, it was not a hard blow, and Hob got his own staff up to meet it the way he had been taught, and the two staves met, and Bram's split.

It split along its whole length, with a sound like the river ice going in spring, a long crack that seemed to go on longer than it should have. Half of it spun away across the dead ground and the other half stayed in Bram's hand, a long splinter, white where the wood had been.

Hob's ears rang. Not the way they rang when he had been hit. It was a high thin ringing like a struck bowl and his teeth ached with it, the way they ached when he bit down on a spoon, and he tasted something that was not blood. Iron. Struck iron, the taste of the smithy when Jorrey put the hot bar in the trough. He stood with his staff up and his mouth open and tasted it.

Nobody said anything.

Bram was looking at the half-staff in his hand. He turned it over. He looked at it the way you would look at something somebody had put in your hand while you were not paying attention, and then he looked at his own hand, and then he put the broken thing down on the ground, carefully, as if it might go off.

"Ash doesn't split like that," Maddoc said.

He said it quietly. He was not looking at the staff. He was looking at Bram, and he went on looking at him for a long moment, and whatever was in his face Hob could not read it, which was unusual, because Maddoc's face generally had nothing in it at all.

Then he said, "Get another," and Bram went and got another from the rack by the gate, and Maddoc said, "Wat, Pim," and the morning went on.

Hob stood where he was for a while with the ringing going out of his ears. He worked it over. He had not won, obviously. He had been on the ground twice and would have been there a third time if the staff had held. But the staff had not held, and he had been standing when it broke, and that was something.

And Bram had stopped. Hob was nearly sure of it. A hair before the staves met, something in Bram had stopped, pulled back, and the blow had come in soft. He had decided to end it. He had looked at Hob bleeding from the mouth and getting up for the third time and he had decided, the way he decided everything, that it was enough, and he had found a way to end it that did not put Hob on the ground again. Hob did not know how a person broke his own staff on purpose. He supposed if anyone could it would be Bram.

He did not know whether to be grateful or insulted. He settled on both.

---

Afterwards they went down to the river, as they always did, and stripped to the waist and washed the dirt off in water that was still cold enough to make Dace shout. The sun had come up properly. The willows dripped. Somebody's dog had followed them down and was investigating the pile of shirts.

"He's going to kill one of us one day," Dace said. He said it cheerfully, wringing out his hair. He had a lump over one eye the size of a plum from Maddoc. "You watch. One of these mornings. Crack, and that's your head, not the stick."

"He's never even hit anyone in the face," Wat said.

"That's what I'm saying. He's saving it."

"He doesn't hit faces because Maddoc told him not to," Pim said. "Third summer. You weren't there. Maddoc took him aside and said something and after that, never the face. Never the head at all."

"Why?"

Pim shrugged, which was the answer to most things about Bram.

Hob rinsed his mouth and spat pink into the current and looked up the bank to where Bram sat alone on the roots of the big willow, boots off, tying something. He had washed already. He washed fast and apart, upstream of everyone else, and nobody had ever said anything about it. He had put his shirt back on. He was looking at his hands again.

Hob went up the bank. He did not know why. The others watched him go and Dace said something and Ansel told him to shut up.

"You'd have stayed down," Hob said, when he got there. He meant it as a joke. It did not come out as one.

Bram looked up at him. "No."

"You would. Third time. Anyone would."

Bram looked at the water. The light came through the willow leaves and moved on his face and did not change it. "You should have," he said.

"That's not the rule."

"I know the rule."

Hob sat down on a root, not too close. The smell was faint here, after the river. "Why'd you stop?"

"Staff broke."

"Before that. You pulled it. I saw you."

"I didn't."

"You did, though. I know what your hard looks like. That wasn't it."

Bram did not answer. He finished tying whatever he was tying, a cord around the top of his boot where the leather had split, and pulled his boot on and stood up, and standing up he was so much bigger than Hob that Hob had to tilt his head.

"Your mouth's still bleeding," Bram said.

"I bit my tongue."

Bram nodded as if that settled something. He picked up his staff, the new one, and looked at it the way he had looked at the broken one, and then he went up the path toward the village without saying anything else.

Hob sat on the root and watched him go. He thought: he does not want to talk about it because he does not want anyone to know he can do that. Pull a blow so fine the other man barely feels it. Break his own staff to end a bout he has already won. He thought: he is so far ahead of the rest of us he has to pretend he is not, or Maddoc would have nothing to teach him and no reason to keep him on the ground, and the ground is the only place anybody in this village stands near him.

It was a good thought. It explained everything. Hob was pleased with it, and he carried it back down the bank and did not look at it again for the rest of the day, which was a shame, because it was wrong in every part.

---

The ferry house stood where the road came down to the water, and it had been built for a ferryman and his wife and had since been asked to hold a ferryman, his wife, six children, the wife's mother, and whatever travelers were stuck on the wrong side when the river came up. Hob was the third child and the second son and he had never, in his life, had a room with a door.

His mother had porridge on when he came in and looked at his mouth and said nothing, which was how she said most things. His grandmother, who was blind and sat by the fire and knew everything, said, "That boy again," and Hob said, "Yes, Nan," and she said, "Hm."

"He pulled it," Hob said. "At the end. I could have been worse."

"Hm."

His father was out on the water already; there was a drover waiting on the far side with a dozen bullocks that would have to be swum across behind the boat, and that was a morning's work and a shilling. Jory, the eldest, was out there with him, learning the ropes, as the saying went, because the ropes were what Jory would get. The ferry had been their great-grandfather's and it went to the eldest. It did not go to the second son. The second son got porridge and a floor to sleep on and the understanding, never spoken, that at some point he would want to be somewhere else.

Hob ate standing up with the little ones hanging off him. He thought about the levy.

Every third or fourth year, when the king in Dunmere needed foot, the levy came up the Ferrin road with a clerk and a sergeant and a wagon and took a number of young men from each village that could spare them, and brought back what was left of them a year or two later. Kell's Ford had been sending its boys to Maddoc for twenty years because of that wagon. Maddoc's boys came back. That was the whole of it. Other villages sent farm boys who had never held anything but a hoe and got them back in pieces or not at all, and Kell's Ford sent boys who knew high guard from low and how to stand in a line and when to go down and, more to the point, when to get up, and the boys came back. Most of them. Enough of them that the village went on paying Maddoc in flour and firewood and never asked what he had done in the wars.

The levy had last come when Hob was twelve. It was overdue. He prayed for it the way other boys prayed for a girl to look at them.

His mother knew. She did not say anything about that either.

"Tobin's boy," she said instead, scraping the pot. "Did he eat, do you think?"

"How would I know?"

"You were with him all morning."

"I was hitting him all morning. It's not the same." He gave his bowl to his sister Nell, who was five and considered the scrapings hers by right. "He looked all right. He always looks all right."

"Hm," said his grandmother.

His mother had a softness for the tanner and his son that Hob had never understood. Everyone else held their noses. But she had been there, the winter Bram was nine and the fever went through the downwind end, and she had been the one who sat with Bram's mother at the end of it, because nobody else would go into that house, and after that Tobin had stopped talking, more or less, and the boy had gone into the pits with him, and that was that. Hob's mother sent bread down there sometimes. Hob carried it. He had never once been asked inside.

"They're alike, those two," his mother said. "You'd never get a word out of either of them with a crowbar. Doesn't mean there's no words in there."

"Bram talks," Hob said. "He said eleven words to me this morning."

"Did you count?"

He had, in fact. He did not say so.

---

The rider came at dusk.

Hob was at the landing because it was his turn to be at the landing, sitting on the upturned skiff with the bell rope in reach in case anyone hailed from the far bank, and watching the light go orange and then grey on the water. His father and Jory had brought the ferry back and gone up to eat. The bullocks were somewhere up the Ferrin road by now, bawling. The river was high for the season and brown with it.

He heard the horse before he saw it, coming down the far bank at a walk, and he stood up and put his hand on the rope and waited to see if it was somebody who would want crossing or somebody who would turn back when they saw the water.

It was somebody who wanted crossing. The rider came down to the landing stage on the far side and sat his horse and raised a hand, and Hob rang the bell twice for his father and shouted "Hold there, we're coming," and his father came down pulling his coat on and swearing about his supper.

It took the usual time. The horse did not like the ferry and the rider did not like the horse not liking it and Hob's father did not like either of them and said so, at length, while he hauled. Hob held the horse's head when they came in and it blew at him and rolled an eye.

The rider was a lean man in a grey coat with a badge sewn on the breast of it: a black heron on a white ground, which was the king's badge, the badge of Caldreth. Hob had seen it on the levy wagon when he was twelve. He had seen it on nothing else, ever. He stared at it and then remembered himself and looked at the man's face instead, which was tired and not unfriendly and had a few days of road on it.

"Is there an inn," the rider said.

"There's the alehouse, sir. Marran's. Up the street on the left. She'll find you a bed."

"Good." He swung down. His legs did not want to hold him for a moment and he steadied himself on the saddle and looked around at the village, what could be seen of it in the dusk, the row of roofs, the church spire that was not much of a spire, the smoke. "Kell's Ford."

"Yes, sir."

"You've a drill ground here. A man who runs it."

Hob's heart did something. "Yes, sir. Sergeant Maddoc. Behind his house, top of the street."

"Sergeant." The rider seemed to find that interesting. "Of what?"

"I don't know, sir. He was in the wars."

"Everyone was in the wars." The rider took a coin out of his coat and gave it to Hob's father, who looked at it and then looked at the rider with more respect than he had shown so far. "I'm riding ahead of a carriage from Dunmere. Two days behind me, perhaps three with the roads. Master Orvane of the king's school, going round the villages that train. Looking at the boys."

"Looking for what?" Hob's father said.

"That's his business." The rider gathered his reins. He had already begun to turn away and then he stopped and looked at Hob, properly, up and down, the way Maddoc looked at a bucket. "You're one of them. One of the sergeant's."

"Yes, sir."

"Any good?"

Hob opened his mouth. He thought about the morning, the dead ground and the white sky and the taste of iron. He thought about the levy wagon and Jory and the ropes and the floor by the fire that he slept on with his brother's foot in his back. He thought about his mother scraping the pot.

"Yes, sir," he said. "I am."

The rider looked at him a moment longer. Then he smiled, not unkindly, the way grown men smiled at a boy who had said something they had once said themselves.

"Maybe," he said. "We'll see." And he led the horse up the street toward Marran's, and the dark took him.

Hob's father watched him go. "School," he said. "What's a school want with ferry boys."

Hob did not answer. He had just noticed something, standing there on the landing with the bell rope still in his hand, and the noticing had gone through him cold.

The man had asked if there was anyone worth looking at. And Hob had said himself. He had said *I am*, and he had not said anything else. He had not said: there is a boy at the downwind end who has never lost a bout in his life. He had not said: there is a boy who broke an ash staff this morning with a blow that could not have broken an egg, and the sergeant looked at him as if he had seen it before. He had stood there with the king's badge in front of him and the whole of his life opening up like a door, and he had kept his mouth shut about Bram.

He had not decided to. He had simply done it. And now that it was done he found he did not want to take it back.

---

He lay awake a long time that night, on the floor with his brother's foot in his back and his grandmother snoring by the banked fire. The river went on outside. He said the words to himself, not aloud, the way he did when he could not sleep.

Never stay down.

He thought about what it meant, the way he always did, and it meant what it always meant, that nothing could be taken from you that you did not give, that the ground was only the end if you let it be. He thought about standing up the third time with blood in his mouth and Pim saying *go on, Hob*. He thought about a carriage on the Ferrin road, two days off, perhaps three, and a man in it who was looking for something, and how he would stand on the dead ground when the man came, and what the man would see.

He did not think about the sound the staff had made. He had decided not to. But it was there anyway, under the river noise, a long crack going on longer than it should have, and the ringing after, and the taste. And Bram standing over the broken halves, looking at his own hand as if it belonged to somebody else.

Hob turned over, and his brother kicked him, and eventually he slept.
